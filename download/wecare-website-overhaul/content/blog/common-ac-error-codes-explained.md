# Common AC Error Codes Explained — What Those Flashing Lights Mean

When your air conditioner's indoor unit suddenly starts blinking a code like E1, E4 or F1 on its display panel, the natural reaction is confusion — followed by reaching for the remote and trying every button. Those error codes are actually a helpful diagnostic system: the AC's control board has detected a specific fault and is telling you, in shorthand, exactly where the problem is. Knowing what each code means saves you time, helps you explain the problem to a technician accurately, and tells you whether the unit is safe to keep running. This guide covers the most common error codes used by Voltas, LG, Samsung, Daikin and similar brands sold in Kashmir, with notes on what to do next.

## Why Kashmir ACs Show Error Codes More Often

Most air conditioners installed in Kashmir run for only three months a year and sit idle for the remaining nine. That long idle period does more harm than continuous use would — dust settles on sensors, lubricants in fan motors thicken and seize, and rubber seals at valve joints dry out and develop slow refrigerant leaks. When the AC is switched on in May after a long winter, the sudden load stresses aged components and the control board detects faults that have been quietly developing since the previous summer. This is why Kashmiri households see more error codes in the first two weeks of the season than homes in Delhi or Mumbai do all year, and why a pre-season service dramatically reduces error code occurrences.

## E1 — Room Temperature Sensor Fault

The E1 code points to the indoor unit's room temperature sensor — a small thermistor mounted behind the front grille that tells the control board the air temperature in your room. A faulty or disconnected sensor sends an impossible reading (usually a fixed -40 or +90 degrees), and the AC shuts down to protect itself. The most common cause in Kashmir is the sensor wire getting pinched during a winter cleaning, or dust on the thermistor tip insulating it from real air temperature. The fix is to clean or replace the sensor — a ten-minute job for a technician, with the part costing around ₹500 to ₹800 depending on brand.

## E2 — Coil Temperature Sensor Fault

E2 indicates a problem with the evaporator coil temperature sensor, which monitors the cold coil inside the indoor unit. When this sensor fails, the AC cannot detect whether the coil is freezing up — a condition that would damage the compressor if it ran unchecked. The unit shuts down and displays E2 to prevent ice from forming on the coil and bursting refrigerant lines. Like E1, this is usually a sensor replacement, not a major repair. If your AC has shown E2 multiple times in the same season, the underlying cause may be low refrigerant causing the coil to over-cool — a technician will check both the sensor and the gas level.

## E3 — Compressor Overload Protection

E3 is a serious code. It means the compressor — the pump that circulates refrigerant through the system — is drawing too much current and the overload protector has tripped to prevent the compressor from burning out. Common causes include dirty outdoor condenser coils (the compressor overheats because it cannot shed heat), low refrigerant (the compressor runs hotter because it is under-lubricated), or a failing compressor itself. In Kashmir, E3 frequently appears after a long winter idle period when the compressor oil has settled and thickened. Switch the AC off immediately, let it sit for an hour, and try once more — if the code returns, call a technician before attempting another restart.

## E4 — Indoor Fan Motor Fault

E4 indicates that the indoor unit's fan motor has stalled, failed, or is not running at the speed the control board is requesting. The fan circulates air across the cold evaporator coil and into your room — without it, the coil freezes within minutes and the AC shuts down to protect itself. Causes include a seized fan motor bearing (very common after a winter of inactivity), a broken fan blade, or a failed control board driver. A technician can usually free a seized bearing in the first visit, but a permanently damaged motor needs replacement. This is one of the more expensive indoor-unit repairs — typically ₹2,500 to ₹4,500 including the part.

## E5 — Outdoor Fan Motor Fault

E5 means the fan in the outdoor condenser unit has failed. The outdoor fan releases the heat the AC has absorbed from inside your home — without it, the compressor overheats and the system shuts down within minutes. In Kashmir, this fan is the most exposed component and accumulates the most dust over the winter. A seized bearing, broken blade, or burnt winding all trigger E5. Switch the AC off immediately and book a technician — running the AC with E5 active will overheat the compressor and turn a fan motor repair into a compressor replacement. The good news is the part itself is reasonably priced.

## F1 and F2 — Refrigerant Leak or Charge Failure

F1 and F2 codes are the most common refrigerant-related error codes across LG, Voltas and Samsung systems. They indicate that the refrigerant pressure has dropped below the safe operating range, almost always due to a leak somewhere in the sealed system. Symptoms include the indoor coil partially freezing, the AC running continuously without reaching the set temperature, and a hissing sound at the indoor unit in rare cases. The fix involves locating the leak (a technician uses electronic leak detectors and soap solution), repairing the joint or pinhole, evacuating the system, and recharging with the correct refrigerant — R32 or R410A depending on the unit.

## P1 — Overvoltage or Power Supply Fault

P1 is a power-protection code. It triggers when the supply voltage to the AC exceeds safe limits — common in Kashmir during stormy weather, after long feeder cuts, or in areas with unstable local supply. The AC protects itself by shutting down rather than risking damage to the control board or compressor. Most modern inverter ACs reset automatically once the voltage returns to a safe range, but if the code persists it usually means the voltage protection circuit itself has failed and needs replacement. A voltage stabiliser with surge protection is strongly recommended for any AC installed in an area with frequent power fluctuations.

## H1 — Defrosting Cycle Active

H1 is the one code that is not a fault — it simply means the outdoor unit is in defrost mode, melting frost that has accumulated on the condenser coil. This happens naturally in cold, humid weather and is most common in spring and autumn when Kashmir mornings are still cold but the AC is being used. The unit pauses cooling for 5 to 15 minutes, melts the frost, and resumes normal operation. No action is required. If H1 appears very frequently or stays active for more than 20 minutes, however, the defrost sensor may be failing — a quick technician visit will confirm.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Should I reset the AC after an error code appears?
Yes, once. Switch the AC off at the remote, then off at the isolator switch or socket, wait two minutes, and switch it back on. If the code reappears within a few minutes, do not keep resetting — the underlying fault will not fix itself and continued resets can cause further damage. See our [AC service page](/services/air-conditioner-repair/) for next steps.

### How do I find my AC's specific error code meaning?
Codes vary slightly by brand. Voltas, LG, Samsung and Daikin all use slightly different numbering. Check the user manual that came with your unit, or search the brand name and code together — most manufacturers publish code lists online. When booking a technician, share the exact code you see.

### What is the most serious AC error code?
E3 (compressor overload) and F1/F2 (refrigerant leak) are the most serious. E3 risks compressor damage if ignored, and refrigerant leaks mean the system cannot cool properly. Either should be addressed the same day to prevent escalation into a full compressor replacement.
